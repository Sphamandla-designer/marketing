## 1. The chain, made legible

### One question, three pieces, three people

The customer asks one question with three parts. Each part lives with a different
person, in a different place, in a different form of handwriting.

| Person | Where | The piece they hold | How we see it | Why it is theirs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Anusha Pillay**, the customer | Property office | The question, and the deadline | A notepad with three blank lines: *Price*, *Material*, *Start*. A wall clock at 17:55 | Her trustees sit at six |
| **Sizwe Mokoena**, the owner | On a roof | **Start date** | A pocket diary: crews and dates in pencil. He underlines *Mon 4 — Thabo* | Only he knows when a crew comes free, because he is standing on the job they are finishing |
| **Lerato Dube**, the administrator | The office | **Price** | She finds the quote and writes the total on a sticky note, which joins a row of others on her monitor | The quote is in her files |
| **Mr Jacobs**, the storeman | The storeroom | **Material** | He counts drums and marks each lid | Stock is physical. Someone has to count it |

Nobody holds all three. That is the entire problem, and it can be read with the
sound off.

### How the viewer follows the chain

1. **The sound travels down the wire.** Anusha hears the hold tune from a
   speakerphone. Cut to Sizwe: the same tune, thinner, through his handset, because
   he is on hold too. Cut to Lerato: a hold light blinks on her desk phone, the
   receiver is at her ear, and what she hears is not music but a man counting. Cut to
   Mr Jacobs: the same counting, now in the room. The audience is led from one end of
   the call to the other by ear.
2. **Eyelines point down the chain.** Anusha, Sizwe and Lerato all face screen-right,
   towards the person they are waiting for. Mr Jacobs, at the end, faces down, at the
   drums. The chain stops with him.
3. **One hand-drawn object per person.** Notepad, diary, sticky note, drum lid. Four
   surfaces, four hands. In the resolution there is one surface.
4. **The blanks never get filled.** When Anusha leaves, her notepad stays on the
   desk with three empty lines.

### The resolution mirrors it

The next caller asks the same three things. Lerato makes three clicks. Each click is
answered by one line on a real screen, in the same order as yesterday's three people
in reverse: price, material, start. The mouse click replaces the hold tune as the
sound of the scene.

## 2. Keeping everyone competent

The film fails if the audience thinks "these people are useless". The rules:

- **Every answer is right first time.** Lerato finds the correct quote. Mr Jacobs
  counts correctly. Sizwe knows his schedule. Nobody searches, panics, apologises or
  opens the wrong file.
- **The whole chain takes three minutes.** The clock goes from 17:55 to 17:58. That
  is a good response by any normal standard. It is simply longer than she has.
- **The answer arrives.** Sizwe gets the complete, correct answer. He gets it a few
  seconds after it stopped mattering. The loss is nobody's fault, which is what makes
  it hurt.
- **Sizwe does not blame anyone.** After the dead line he puts the phone back to his
  ear and says, "Thanks, Lerato." That is the character.
- **The work is visibly good.** Straight seams on the roof. An orderly store. A tidy
  desk. We respect this business.
- **Lerato is not replaced by software.** She gives the answer on day two. The
  product makes her the hero of the film.

The emotion is in one place: a capable man on a roof holding the right answer and
nobody to give it to.

## 6. Production plan

### The governing rule

**No character exists in two worlds.** Lerato is filmed. Sizwe, Anusha and Mr Jacobs
are generated. Nobody has to match across the two methods, so the hardest continuity
problem in hybrid production never arises.

Lerato is the one to film because her scenes carry the product, the real screen, the
most hands-on action and the last line, and because they all happen at one desk.

### Workstreams

**A. Practical filming** — one office set, one actor, one day

| Item | Shots |
| --- | --- |
| Lerato at her desk, afternoon set-up: hold light, printed quote, sticky note | 5 |
| Lerato at her desk, morning set-up: bare monitor edge, mug, ringing phone | 9, 11, 16 |
| Finger above the HOLD key | 10 |
| Monitor filmed live with the real product running, operated by the actor | 11, and as plates for 12–14 |
| Tabletop inserts with hand doubles: notepad and pen, pocket diary and pencil | 2, 4 |

The two tabletop inserts are filmed although their owners are generated. They are
hands, paper and legible handwriting, which is where generated footage is least
reliable. Hand doubles are matched for skin tone, age and sleeve.

**B. Real product screen captures**

- Built from the clean example account (V7) on a desktop browser at full width.
- Captured both as screen recordings of the real clicks and as high-resolution stills
  of each end state.
- Shots 12, 13 and 14 are these captures, cropped to one row and finished as screen
  inserts with a slight monitor texture so they sit with the filmed shots.
- No interface element is ever generated, redrawn, re-typeset or animated.

**C. AI-generated footage**

| Location | Shots | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Property office | 1, 7 | One character, one angle. Shot 7 needs her to press a key and exit; the key press may be cut as a separate filmed insert if the hand fails |
| Roof | 3, 8, 15 | One master frame fixes the geography. Shot 8 carries the sync line |
| Storeroom | 6 | One character, one angle, counting |

Six generated shots in three locations. Each character is confined to a single
location and a single costume.

**D. Compositing**

- The wall clock face, at 17:55 and 17:58.
- Phone displays, wherever one is visible.
- The competitor's quote: a designed, fictitious letterhead.
- The contractor's name on workwear and on the bakkie, if seen.
- Handheld drift on shot 8, added to a stable plate.
- The end card, built from the supplied brand files: navy `#0F172A`, teal `#00BFA6`,
  the Outfit typeface, the horizontal logo without tagline.
- Any other legible text in a generated shot is replaced or kept out of focus.

**E. Sound**

| Element | How |
| --- | --- |
| Hold tune | One original eight-bar piece, written to stop on an unresolved note at bar six. Owned outright by the brand. Not a library hold track and not a soundalike |
| Worldising | The tune is played through a real desk speakerphone and a real mobile handset and re-recorded for shots 1, 3 and 7 |
| Counting | Mr Jacobs's count is recorded once and used twice: clean for shot 6, and played down a real phone line for shot 5 |
| Voices | South African voice actors, recorded before any sync shot is generated. Lerato's actor records her own lines on set and in a booth |
| Phone voices | Processed from clean recordings |
| Ambience and foley | Wind, gas torch, air-conditioning, fluorescent hum, fridge, radio, marker, pencil, mouse, keys. Recorded or library, laid by hand |
| End sting | The tune's phrase resolved by a three-piece band, recorded live |
| Mix | Broadcast loudness for television; a separate mix for social, where the opening tune must read on a phone speaker |

No audio produced by a video-generation tool is used anywhere.

**F. Editing and finishing**

- A sound-only cut is built first: tune, counting, voices and voiceover on a
  45-second timeline. If the story works with eyes closed, picture is cut to it.
- Offline edit, then the 30 and 15 cut as their own films from the same rushes.
- One grade across filmed and generated shots, with shared grain and a common
  highlight response applied last, so that all footage passes through the same
  finish.
- Conform to 25 fps. Generated footage that arrives at 24 fps is conformed, not
  frame-blended.
- Deliverables: 45, 30 and 15, each in 16:9 and 9:16, with and without subtitles.

### Order of work

1. GEMIS signs off V1 to V9 and builds the example account.
2. The three tests in section 7.
3. Script lock. Casting of Lerato and the voices.
4. Tune composed. Voices recorded. Sound-only cut approved.
5. Screen captures.
6. Practical shoot.
7. Generation, using frames from the practical shoot as the look reference.
8. Compositing, edit, mix, grade.
9. Versions and delivery.

Generation comes after the shoot so that the generated shots are matched to real
photography, not the other way round.

## 7. The three shots to test first

Each test has a pass condition and a fallback. None of the three is safe to assume.

### Test 1 — Shot 8: Sizwe, close, "Mrs Pillay? …Hello? …Thanks, Lerato."

*Why:* the emotional centre of the film, the longest shot, and the only generated
shot with meaningful lip-sync. It also has to match shot 3 as the same man on the
same roof in the same light.

*Make:* shot 3 and shot 8 together, from one character reference and one roof master
frame, with sync driven by the recorded voice.

*Pass if:* viewers shown the two shots cold say it is the same man; the three short
lines sync at television size; and the performance is a man standing still, not a
man acting sad. Skin, eyes and hands survive six and a half seconds without drift.

*Fallback:* play "Mrs Pillay? …Hello?" on the back of his head and shoulder and
return to his face only for the silence. If the face itself does not hold, Sizwe
becomes a filmed character and the roof becomes a location shoot.

### Test 2 — Shots 11 to 14: the product on screen

*Why:* it is the brand's proof. If the audience cannot read the figure, the film has
no resolution.

*Make:* the over-shoulder shot filmed off a real monitor running the example
account, and the three cropped inserts, cut to Lerato's recorded lines at the
scripted durations.

*Pass if:* at television viewing distance, and in the 9:16 version on a phone, a
viewer can read the Grand Total, the stock figure and the start date in the time
each is on screen, and can say afterwards what she looked up. No moiré or flicker.

*Fallback:* tighten each crop further, lengthen the three inserts by borrowing from
shot 15, or reduce to two inserts as in the 15-second version.

### Test 3 — Shot 6: Mr Jacobs counting drums

*Why:* it combines the things generated footage handles worst: hands, a marker
making a mark, rows of near-identical objects that must not change in number, and a
spoken count. It is also the end of the chain; if it is unconvincing the idea
collapses.

*Make:* the shot at full four seconds with the count laid from the voice recording.

*Pass if:* the drums stay put, his hand touches a lid and leaves a tick that
persists, and nothing in the racking shifts between frames.

*Fallback:* film it. A storeroom, one actor and a marker is an inexpensive set-up,
and it could share the practical day. In that case Mr Jacobs joins Lerato as a
filmed character.

### One further check

Before any of the above, assemble the first eighteen seconds as stills and sound
only, and play it to five people who know nothing about the film. Ask who is waiting
for whom, and for what. If they cannot answer, the chain is fixed on paper before
money is spent on pictures.

### Camera language

- **Format.** 16:9, 25 fps, spherical lenses, normal shutter.
- **Lenses.** Portraits on a 50 mm equivalent at eye level. Inserts on a 100 mm
  macro. Over-shoulder on a 35 mm. Shot 8 on an 85 mm.
- **Movement.** Locked-off, with two exceptions: the slow push-in on shot 3 and the
  handheld drift on shot 8. No drone, no orbit, no tracking through doors, no whip
  pans, no speed changes.
- **Framing.** Part one: every waiting character sits left of centre, facing
  screen-right into empty space. Mr Jacobs is centred, looking down. Part two: Lerato
  faces camera, three-quarter. She is no longer looking off towards someone else.
- **Depth.** Moderate. Backgrounds are soft but readable. No extreme shallow focus
  and no blooming highlights.
- **Shot size.** The four portraits in part one match: head and shoulders with the
  hands and the handwritten object in frame.
- **Length.** No shot under one second. No generated shot over six and a half.

### Colour and grade

- **Palette.** Day one is warm and slightly dusty: tan, navy, concrete grey, hi-vis
  orange, the yellow of sticky notes. Day two is cleaner and half a stop brighter,
  with whites that are properly white.
- **Brand colour.** ManaGEM teal and sky-blue appear only on the product screen and
  the end card. They are in nothing else: no wardrobe, no props, no signage.
- **Skin.** Protected in every shot and graded per person, not per scene. Rich,
  true skin tones are the first test of whether the film looks real.
- **Contrast.** Photographic. Blacks sit just above zero. Highlights roll off; no
  clipping on the roof.
- **Texture.** Fine grain over everything at the same strength, applied after the
  grade, with a trace of halation on the hardest highlights. This is what makes
  filmed and generated shots belong to one roll.
- **Not permitted.** Teal-and-orange grading, crushed blacks, glow, lens flares added
  in post, vignettes, sharpening.
